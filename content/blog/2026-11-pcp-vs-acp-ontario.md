## Two stripes or one

It's a chest pain call with a mixed crew. One paramedic has one stripe on the epaulette, the other has two. Both are fully certified. Both work to the same provincial standards. But only one of them can give certain medications or perform certain procedures, and that decides who rides in the back.

So the PCP vs ACP question in Ontario comes down to three things: the same standards, a different authorized scope, and written rules about who attends.

## What each one is

A Primary Care Paramedic (PCP) is the entry-to-practice level. Training is a two-year community college diploma; the Peel Paramedic Association describes it as about 2,000 hours of classroom and field clinical time. Graduates write the ministry's AEMCA exam.

An Advanced Care Paramedic (ACP) completes a further program after the PCP diploma and field experience. The Peel Paramedic Association describes ACP training as about 3,200 hours in total, with at least two years of paramedic experience required to apply, followed by hospital clinical time and months of preceptorship.

Critical Care Paramedics (CCP) are a third level, mostly in transport and flight roles, with a scope wider again than ACP. They're outside this post.

## Scope of practice: what changes

Scope in Ontario comes from the Advanced Life Support Patient Care Standards (version 5.4 has been in force since 2 June 2025) and from your base hospital's authorization. Both levels work to the Basic Life Support Patient Care Standards.

The table below is a summary drawn from the Peel Paramedic Association's published scope page. Lists vary by base hospital and by auxiliary directives your service has adopted, so treat it as an overview, not your authorization.

| | PCP | ACP |
|---|---|---|
| Training (per Peel) | ~2,000 hours, 2-year diploma | ~3,200 hours total, post-diploma |
| Insignia | One stripe | Two stripes |
| ECG | 12- and 15-lead acquisition, STEMI identification | Adds rhythm-based electrical therapy (cardioversion, pacing) |
| Airway | Supraglottic airway, CPAP, capnometry | Adds endotracheal intubation and needle cricothyrotomy |
| Medications | A core set including ASA, nitroglycerin, salbutamol, epinephrine, naloxone, ondansetron | The PCP set plus drugs such as amiodarone, adenosine, fentanyl, ketamine and midazolam |
| Vascular access | IV where certified | IV, and IO routes |
| Chest | Not in scope | Needle thoracostomy |

The ACP scope adds invasive procedures and drugs that need closer titration or carry more risk. Exact medications, doses and conditions come from the directives, not from a summary table.

## Who attends in a mixed crew

New paramedics ask about this rule more than any other, and Ontario base hospitals put it in writing. The Southwest Ontario Regional Base Hospital Program's crew configuration policy is a clear example.

The policy says "the ACP MUST attend any patient when the patient has received, requires, or is anticipated to require an intervention or treatment beyond the PCP scope of practice." The PCP may attend a patient who:

- doesn't need treatment beyond PCP scope at the point of transport,
- hasn't received treatment beyond PCP scope before transport,
- is improving after treatment that's within PCP scope, or
- isn't expected to need treatment beyond PCP scope.

Two more points from the same policy matter on the road. An ACP crew shouldn't perform ACP directives and then hand the patient to a PCP crew for transport unless there are extenuating circumstances, and those must be reported. During offload delay at hospital, care can pass from an attending ACP to a PCP crew only if no treatment beyond PCP scope has been given.

The word that does the work is **anticipated**. A patient who's stable now but likely to deteriorate is an ACP patient. Recognising that early is a clinical judgment, and it's one of the hardest calls in a mixed crew.

## Certification and authorization

Ontario separates the certificate from the permission to practise.

- The Ministry of Health runs the certification exams and issues the AEMCA and ACP certificates. The ministry says these certificates don't expire.
- To actually perform controlled acts, a paramedic needs the right ministry certificate, employment with a certified ambulance service, and authorization from the medical director of an Ontario regional base hospital program.
- Base hospitals keep that authorization current through continuing medical education and quality review. ACPs usually carry heavier annual CME requirements than PCPs.

A degree doesn't change any of this. Your scope on day one depends on your certification level and your base hospital's authorization, not on whether you hold a diploma or a degree.

## Choosing a path

If you're a student or new PCP deciding whether to pursue ACP, ask yourself:

1. Do you want the high-acuity calls? ACP work means more of them and more responsibility in the back.
2. Can you commit to the CME? ACP authorization comes with more mandatory training each year.
3. What does your service need? ACP positions, and support for ACP education, vary a lot between services.

## The takeaway

PCPs and ACPs share standards but not scope. The ACP adds invasive procedures and higher-risk medications. In a mixed crew, base hospital policy says the ACP attends any patient who needs, or is expected to need, care beyond PCP scope, and the hardest part is spotting "expected to" early.
